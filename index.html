1. What is Information Retrieval?

Information Retrieval (IR) means: a person needs some information, there is a huge pile of documents, and a computer has to find the few documents that help, and show them in the best order.

Your professor's definition (Lecture 1, from the Stanford textbook): IR is finding material (usually documents) of an unstructured nature (usually text) that satisfies an information need from within large collections (usually stored on computers).

Break that sentence into its words:

  • Material / documents: the things being searched. A web page, a PDF, an email, a tweet, a product description.
  • Unstructured: not arranged in neat rows and columns like an Excel sheet. Normal free text, written for humans.
  • Information need: what the person actually wants to know, inside their head.
  • Large collection: millions or billions of documents, far too many to read one by one.
AnalogyYou walk into a giant library with no catalogue and ask the librarian "something on cheap laptops for college". A great librarian understands what you mean (even if the books say "affordable notebooks"), pulls out the 10 best books from millions, and hands you the best one first. IR is the science of building that librarian as software. Google, Amazon search, Google Scholar and the retrieval step in ChatGPT-style RAG systems are all IR systems.

Why is it hard? Lecture 1 does a small calculation: Google has about 100 billion pages. If checking one page took just 1 millisecond, checking all of them for one query would take 100,000,000 seconds, about 3.17 years. So we cannot read every document per query. We must prepare in advance (build an index) and score cleverly (rank by relevance). That one problem drives the whole course.

IR is also different from a database. A database (SQL) works on structured tables, finds exact matches and gives one precise answer. IR works on messy text, does approximate matching, and returns a ranked list sorted by how relevant each document probably is.

6. Ideas that repeat across lectures

These patterns show up again and again. Spotting them makes the later lectures much easier and they are favourite MCQ material.

"Independence" is assumed three times
BIM (L10), Naive Bayes (L13) and the unigram language model (L11, L12) all pretend each word appears independently of the others. It is false in real language ("New" and "York" go together) but it makes the math easy and still works well.
Smoothing appears four times
Whenever a count can be zero, a probability becomes zero and kills the whole product. Fixes: +0.5 in BIM/BM25 (L10), add-1 Laplace for bigrams (L11), Laplace / Jelinek-Mercer / Dirichlet for query likelihood (L12), add-1 for Naive Bayes (L13).
"Sum over query terms" is the same shape everywhere
TF-IDF score, BIM's RSV, BM25 and log query likelihood all end up as: for each query word found in the document, add up some weight. Three different theories, one computation. This is why one inverted index serves all of them.
Logs are used to avoid tiny numbers
Multiplying many probabilities like 0.001 gives numbers too small for a computer (underflow). Taking logs turns multiply into add. Seen in L11 (n-grams), L12 and L13 (Naive Bayes).
Rare words matter more
IDF (L6), BIM's term weight (L10) and the "IDF-like term" in BM25 (L10) all say: a word that appears in few documents tells you more. "arachnocentric" is a stronger clue than "the".
The same toy examples come back
Shakespeare plays (Brutus, Caesar, Calpurnia) in L5, L6, L10. The hug / pug / pun / bun / hugs corpus in BPE, WordPiece and Unigram (L4). Chinese / Tokyo / Japan in both Naive Bayes models (L13). Practice these exact examples.
Precision vs recall is a trade-off
Stemming, query expansion and feedback usually raise recall (find more relevant documents) but can lower precision (also pull in junk). This trade-off is mentioned in L5, L7, L8 and L9.
The Markov idea appears twice
"The next step depends only on where you are now." N-gram language models (L11) predict the next word from the last one or two words; the PageRank random surfer (L14) picks the next page using only the current page. Both are Markov chains.
Every model is a "BoW" model
Bag of words: word order is thrown away, only counts (or presence) matter. "John is quicker than Mary" and "Mary is quicker than John" look identical. True for VSM, BIM, BM25, unigram LM and Naive Bayes.
